1. Renal Medullary Interstitial Osmolarity Gradient

Enrichment

Demonstrating how the thin descending limb (water permeable) and thick ascending limb (active NKCC2 solute transport) build the osmotic gradient from cortex to inner medulla. This level of transport detail goes beyond SLO 15.6.7 but helps visualise why the loop of Henle is shaped the way it is.

Beyond the syllabus, but useful context: Inhibiting the NKCC2 co-transporter (e.g., via loop diuretics) flattens this gradient, causing osmotic water loss and diuresis.

2. Plasma ADH Secretion Threshold Curve

SLO 15.6.9–15.6.10

Shows hypothalamic osmoreceptor responsiveness. Below ~280 mOsm/kg, ADH secretion is minimal; above ~285 mOsm/kg, secretion rises sharply as the body works to conserve water.

Key Takeaway (SLO 15.6.10): During dehydration, plasma osmolarity rises, driving ADH up and producing small volumes of concentrated urine. After excess water intake, osmolarity falls, ADH drops, and urine becomes dilute and abundant.

3. Thermal Strategies: Endotherms vs Ectotherms

SLO 15.9.1–15.9.2

Comparative metabolic rate profiles: homeotherms (endothermic) hold a steady rate across a comfortable range of temperatures, while poikilotherms (ectothermic) show metabolic rate scaling directly with ambient temperature.

Key Takeaway: Endotherms expend extra metabolic energy on physiological effectors (shivering, sweating) outside their comfortable range, while ectotherms simply track the environment's temperature.

🧠 2. Nephron Structure & Function (SLO 15.6.6–15.6.8)

Nephron Part Capillary Bed Primary Function
Renal Corpuscle (Glomerulus + Bowman's Capsule) Glomerular capillaries The glomerulus is a high-pressure, leaky capillary tuft — simple filtration of blood plasma; cells and large proteins are retained, everything else passes into Bowman's capsule as filtrate
Proximal Convoluted Tubule (PCT) Peritubular capillaries Reabsorption of most water, ions and nutrients
Loop of Henle Vasa Recta Loop of Henle actively builds the medullary osmotic gradient (countercurrent multiplier); the vasa recta run alongside it in the same hairpin shape and passively pick up/release water & solutes to preserve that gradient (countercurrent exchanger) without washing it away
Distal Convoluted Tubule & Collecting Duct Peritubular capillaries Reabsorption and secretion, fine-tuned by ADH, aldosterone & PTH

Loop of Henle + vasa recta in osmoregulation

The loop of Henle is the active partner: the ascending limb pumps out Na⁺/K⁺/2Cl⁻ (impermeable to water) while the descending limb lets water leave (AQP-1), and this counter-flow arrangement multiplies a small difference into the steep ~300→1200 mOsm/L gradient running from cortex to inner medulla. The vasa recta are the capillaries that supply this region — and they run in the exact same hairpin shape right alongside the loop. As blood descends into the medulla it loses water and gains solutes from the interstitium; as it ascends back out it does the reverse. Because this exchange happens passively and symmetrically along both limbs, blood leaving the vasa recta carries away only slightly more water/solute than it brought in — so the capillaries deliver oxygen and nutrients to the medulla without flushing out the gradient the loop worked to build. Without this countercurrent exchange design, ordinary straight capillaries would simply wash the gradient away, and the kidney would lose its ability to concentrate urine.

🩸 2b. Glomerulus vs Peritubular Capillaries vs Vasa Recta

Capillary Bed Location Pressure / Behaviour Role
Glomerular capillaries Renal corpuscle (cortex) High hydrostatic pressure; fenestrated & leaky Bulk filtration of plasma into Bowman's capsule
Peritubular capillaries Surround PCT & DCT (cortex) Low pressure; well suited to absorb reclaimed fluid Reabsorption from tubule & secretion into tubule
Vasa recta Alongside the loop of Henle (medulla) Low pressure; hairpin-shaped, runs parallel & countercurrent to tubular flow Passive countercurrent exchange — supplies the medulla with blood while preserving the osmotic gradient

⚖ 3. Hormonal Regulation of Kidney Function (SLO 15.6.9)

Hormone Origin / Trigger Effect on Kidney
Antidiuretic Hormone (ADH) Posterior pituitary — rising blood osmolarity / dehydration Increases collecting duct water reabsorption → concentrated urine
Aldosterone Adrenal cortex — low blood Na⁺ or low blood volume Increases Na⁺ reabsorption & K⁺ secretion in distal tubule/collecting duct
Parathyroid Hormone (PTH) Parathyroid glands — low blood calcium Increases calcium reabsorption in renal tubules

🏥 4. Kidney Diseases Quick Reference (SLO 15.7)

Condition Cause / Description Management
Cystitis / Urethritis / Pyelonephritis Bacterial UTI of the bladder, urethra, or kidney respectively (e.g. E. coli); pyelonephritis is the most serious, with fever & flank pain Antibiotics, increased fluid intake
Kidney Stones Crystallisation of minerals (e.g. calcium oxalate, uric acid), often linked to dehydration Hydration, medication, lithotripsy, or surgery for larger stones
Kidney Failure Loss of kidney function, e.g. from diabetes or hypertension Haemodialysis (external machine) or peritoneal dialysis (internal lining)
Transplant Rejection Risk Immune system attacks a transplanted kidney if blood/tissue compatibility is poor Immunosuppressant drugs (raise infection & cancer risk)

🔄 5. Positive vs Negative Feedback (SLO 15.2)

Type Definition AKU-EB Named Examples
Positive Feedback Response amplifies / intensifies the original stimulus, driving the process toward completion Blood clotting; childbirth/labour contractions; lactation (milk ejection reflex)
Negative Feedback Response reverses/counteracts the change to restore the original set-point Blood glucose regulation (insulin & glucagon)

🌿 6. Plant Osmoregulatory Adaptations (SLO 15.3.1)

Plant Group Habitat Key Adaptations
Hydrophyte Submerged or floating in water Thin cuticle, reduced roots, air-filled aerenchyma for buoyancy
Halophyte Saline soils / coastal marshes Salt-excreting glands, succulent water-storing tissue
Mesophyte Moderate, well-watered environments Average leaf size and cuticle thickness; no extreme adaptations needed
Xerophyte Arid / desert environments Thick cuticle, sunken stomata, reduced leaf area, extensive root systems
